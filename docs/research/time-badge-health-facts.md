# Health facts for the time badges

Ticket: SYR-21. Retrieved 2026-09-26.

This document reports what health agencies and peer-reviewed papers say happens in the body
after the last cigarette, one fact per time badge. It is not medical advice and makes no
promise: every figure is an average or a range from the cited source, and bodies differ. The
French wording below attributes or hedges each fact ("selon l'OMS", "en général", "les sources
indiquent"). One point applies to every badge: the user is on a nicotine patch, so any claim
about nicotine leaving the body is false for them and is excluded.

The sources time every fact from the last cigarette smoked. Time badges key on the streak
(`CONTEXT.md`), which a slip does not restart, so after a slip the two clocks diverge. Badges
are permanent and the copy reports what sources say, not the user's current state, so the badge
keeps its fact; nothing in this document should drive a timer off the last cigarette.

## Facts per badge

### 20 minutes

- **Fact.** Heart rate and blood pressure fall back as the acute rise from the last cigarette
  wears off.
- **French.** « Selon l'OMS, 20 min après, le rythme cardiaque et la tension baissent. »
- **Sources.** A: "Within 20 minutes, your heart rate and blood pressure drop." G: "Acutely, BP
  rises transiently within minutes and starts to decrease back to baseline by 15 minutes after
  smoking a cigarette." C: "A few minutes after quitting — Your heart rate drops."
- **Confidence: medium.** Agencies agree; G supports the time course. H, the study WHO cites,
  only measured the rise for 15 minutes after one cigarette and never states a 20-minute
  return. Patch nicotine keeps some of the heart-rate effect, hence "baissent", not "redeviennent
  normales" (F's wording).

### 8 hours

- **Fact.** The carbon monoxide level in the blood is roughly halved.
- **French.** « Les sources indiquent que le monoxyde de carbone du sang a baissé de moitié environ. »
- **Sources.** F: "8 heures après la dernière cigarette — La quantité de monoxyde de carbone dans
  le sang diminue de moitié." I: "CO's typical 4-hr half-life makes it more sensitive for
  verifying 24-hr abstinence." J: "NBO2 reduces the elimination half-life of CO from 320 minutes
  in room air to 74 minutes".
- **Confidence: medium.** The 8 h claim itself is only in F (secondary). The half-life papers
  (4 h in I, about 5 h in J) make "at least halved by 8 h" consistent, but that link is our
  arithmetic, not a sentence in a source. F's second claim, "L'oxygénation des cellules redevient
  normale", has no source found and is dropped.

### 24 hours

- **Fact.** WHO puts the blood carbon monoxide level back to normal by 12 hours.
- **French.** « Selon l'OMS, le monoxyde de carbone du sang est revenu à un niveau normal. »
- **Sources.** A: "12 hours, the carbon monoxide level in your blood drops to normal." C: "24
  hours to a few days after quitting — […] The carbon monoxide level in your blood drops to
  normal." B: "Several days — Carbon monoxide level in the blood drops to level of someone who
  does not smoke."
- **Confidence: medium.** Agencies disagree on timing (12 h, 24 h to a few days, several days).
  At 24 h the claim holds for WHO and the lower bound of ACS only, hence the attribution to WHO.
  F's 24 h claims are rejected, see Gaps.

### 48 hours

- **Fact.** Taste and smell start to improve within days or weeks.
- **French.** « Goût et odorat : les sources notent un mieux dans les jours ou semaines. »
- **Sources.** D: "You'll notice some benefits within days or weeks: your senses of taste and
  smell improve". F: "48 heures après la dernière cigarette — Le goût et l'odorat s'améliorent."
- **Confidence: low.** The 48 h figure appears only in F. The one primary study found (K) first
  measures taste recovery at 2 weeks. See Gaps.

### 72 hours

- **Fact.** Breathing starts to feel easier within days or weeks.
- **French.** « Selon le NHS, respirer devient souvent plus facile en quelques jours ou semaines. »
- **Sources.** D: "You'll notice some benefits within days or weeks: […] you start to breathe
  more easily". F: "72 heures après la dernière cigarette — Respirer devient plus facile. Les
  bronches commencent à se relâcher".
- **Confidence: low.** The 72 h timing and the bronchi claim come only from F. D gives a direction
  but a vague window. See Gaps.

### 1 week

- **Fact.** The carbon monoxide level in the blood is back to that of a non-smoker.
- **French.** « Monoxyde de carbone du sang : niveau d'un non-fumeur, selon les agences de santé. »
- **Sources.** A: "12 hours, the carbon monoxide level in your blood drops to normal." C: "24
  hours to a few days after quitting — […] The carbon monoxide level in your blood drops to
  normal." B: "Several days — Carbon monoxide level in the blood drops to level of someone who
  does not smoke."
- **Confidence: high.** Three agencies agree, and each gives a window ending before day 7
  ("several days" in B is the latest). No source names day 7 itself, so the badge reports a
  change already reached, not one that happens that day. Unaffected by the patch: nicotine
  replacement does not deliver carbon monoxide.

### 2 weeks

- **Fact.** In one study, taste sensitivity on the tip and edges of the tongue had recovered
  after 2 weeks, in people using nicotine patches.
- **French.** « Dans une étude, le goût revenait au bout de 2 semaines, patch compris. »
- **Sources.** K: "After 2 weeks a recovery could be observed on the 3 Tip and the 2 edge loci;
  the recovery in the posterior loci was complete after 9 weeks". K's methods: "a nicotine
  replacement therapy prescription was proposed. This prescription was given for 3 months after
  the day of cessation in doses adapted to the patient, usually decreasing from 21 mg to 7 mg
  over 3 months."
- **Confidence: medium.** Peer-reviewed and measured, and the patch taper matches the user's,
  but the follow-up group is small (24 people) and it is a single study.

### 1 month

- **Fact.** Circulation improves and lung function increases between 2 and 12 weeks.
- **French.** « Selon l'OMS, circulation et fonction pulmonaire s'améliorent entre 2 et 12 semaines. »
- **Sources.** A: "2-12 weeks, your circulation improves and your lung function increases." D:
  "better blood circulation to your heart and muscles, which will make physical activity
  easier".
- **Confidence: medium.** Primary agency, anchored in the 1990 Surgeon General report it cites;
  the page does not say which study backs "2-12 weeks". F gives a close window ("2 semaines à 3
  mois").

### 3 months

- **Fact.** Lung function improves by about 5 % within a few months, in people without chronic
  lung disease.
- **French.** « Selon un rapport américain de référence, le souffle gagne environ 5 % en quelques mois. »
- **Sources.** E: "For persons without overt chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD),
  smoking cessation improves pulmonary function about 5 percent within a few months after
  cessation." A: "2-12 weeks, your circulation improves and your lung function increases."
- **Confidence: high.** Primary report (Surgeon General 1990 executive summary), consistent with
  WHO. "Le souffle" simplifies "pulmonary function"; "fonction pulmonaire" is the literal term if
  preferred.

### 6 months

- **Fact.** Coughing and shortness of breath decrease over the first months, mostly within the
  first year.
- **French.** « Selon l'OMS, toux et essoufflement diminuent en général entre 1 et 9 mois. »
- **Sources.** A: "1-9 months, coughing and shortness of breath decrease." C and B: "1 to 12
  months — Coughing and shortness of breath decrease." M: "Changes in symptoms occurred primarily
  in the first year after smoking cessation."
- **Confidence: high.** Three agencies agree, and a large randomised trial (M, 5,887 smokers
  with early COPD) supports the first-year timing.

### 1 year

- **Fact.** The excess risk of coronary heart disease caused by smoking is reduced by about half
  after one year.
- **French.** « Après 1 an, les sources notent une nette baisse du surrisque cardiaque lié au tabac. »
- **Sources.** E: "The excess risk of CHD caused by smoking is reduced by about half after 1 year
  of smoking abstinence and then declines gradually." A: "1 year, your risk of coronary heart
  disease is about half that of a smoker's." D: "after 1 year, risk of heart attack halves
  compared to a smoker's". B: "1 to 2 years — Risk of heart attack drops sharply." and "3 to 6
  years — Added risk of coronary heart disease drops by half." N, summarising the 2020 report:
  "risk decreasing rapidly in the period immediately following cessation, along with a slower
  long-term decline".
- **Confidence: medium.** "About half at 1 year" rests on the 1990 report; the CDC's 2024 page,
  after the 2020 report, moves "drops by half" to 3–6 years. Hence "nettement baissé" instead of
  "de moitié". WHO and NHS also say "risk halved", which is not the same claim as E's "excess
  risk halved".

## Gaps

- **20 min: WHO's anchor is indirect.** H shows a cigarette raises blood pressure and arterial
  stiffness over the following 15 minutes; it does not measure a return to baseline at 20. G
  gives the return ("by 15 minutes"). On a patch, nicotine keeps some heart-rate effect, so the
  wording avoids "back to normal".
- **8 h vs 12 h vs days for carbon monoxide.** F: halved at 8 h. WHO (A): normal at 12 h. ACS
  (C): normal at 24 h to a few days. CDC (B): non-smoker level after several days. Half-life
  papers (I, J; J is about poisoning, not smokers) give about 4–5 h, which fits F and A. The
  8 h badge keeps "halved"; the 24 h badge attributes "normal" to WHO; the 1 week badge states
  "non-smoker level", the one wording every source supports.
- **24 h: F's claims are rejected.** "Le risque d'infarctus du myocarde diminue déjà": no primary
  source found; A, B, C, D and E give no 24 h cardiac claim. "Le corps ne contient plus de
  nicotine" (also C, B: "Nicotine level in the blood drops to zero"): false for a patch user.
  "Les poumons commencent à éliminer le mucus": no source found. Replaced by the carbon monoxide
  fact.
- **48 h: no primary source for taste and smell.** Only F gives 48 h; NHS (D) says "within days
  or weeks"; the only measured data (K) show taste recovery from 2 weeks. Proposed: keep the
  hedged "jours ou semaines" copy (taste already has its measured fact at 2 weeks).
- **72 h: no primary source.** "Respirer devient plus facile / les bronches se relâchent" traces
  only to F; NHS (D) supports the direction with a vague window. The popular list trail ends in
  public-health pages without a study. The carbon monoxide agreement point is used at 1 week,
  so the hedged NHS wording stays.
- **1 week: no change is timed at day 7.** No agency lists a 7-day change. The withdrawal time
  course was rejected: Hughes 2007 says symptoms "peak within the first week and last 2–4
  weeks" (https://academic.oup.com/ntr/article-pdf/9/3/315/3842403/9-3-315.pdf), but a patch
  damps withdrawal, so that curve does not describe this user, and it is not a health gain. The
  badge uses the carbon monoxide "non-smoker level" fact instead, on which A, B and C all agree
  by one week.
- **1 month: range, not a point.** WHO's "2-12 weeks" is a window; no source ties a change to
  day 30.
- **6 months: range, not a point.** "1-9 months" (A) and "1 to 12 months" (B, C) are windows.
  The "cils bronchiques repoussent" claim (F, 1–9 months) has no primary source found and is not
  used.
- **1 year: sources disagree on "half".** 1990 report, WHO and NHS: about half at 1 year. CDC
  2024: "drops sharply" at 1–2 years, halved at 3–6 years. The 2020 report text itself could not
  be read (HHS PDFs returned 403, NCBI Bookshelf a captcha); its conclusion is quoted from a
  secondary summary (N). F's "Le risque d'accident vasculaire cérébral rejoint celui d'un
  non-fumeur" at 1 year contradicts A ("5 to 15 years") and is not used.
